## Historical Context

### Authorship, date, and provenance

The attribution to **Odo of Tournai, bishop of Cambrai**, is unusually strong for an early-twelfth-century theological opusculum. The prologue speaks in the first person as a bishop expelled from Cambrai because he refused to receive from the emperor the staff and ring already received at ecclesiastical consecration. A twelfth-century manuscript from Anchin identifies the text as *“Tractatus domni Odonis, Cameracensis episcopi, quæ sit blasphemia Spiritus.”* There is no substantial modern case for pseudonymity or misattribution. (<a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="10&lt;sup&gt;o&lt;/sup&gt; (Fol. 112 v&lt;sup&gt;o&lt;/sup&gt;). « Tractatus domni Odonis, Cameracensis episcopi, quæ sit blasphemia Spiritus »">[1]</a>; <a class="cite" href="#ref-4" title="Patrologiae cursus completus">[4]</a>). <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="10&lt;sup&gt;o&lt;/sup&gt; (Fol. 112 v&lt;sup&gt;o&lt;/sup&gt;). « Tractatus domni Odonis, Cameracensis episcopi, quæ sit blasphemia Spiritus »">[1]</a>

The safest date is **during Odo’s extended residence at Anchin, approximately 1108–1112**, rather than the often-repeated but overly precise “1110–1113.” Harald Sellner’s reconstruction, following Irven Resnick, places Odo at Anchin from early 1108 until 1112, followed by a brief return to Cambrai; Odo was back at Anchin in 1113 because of serious illness. The treatise itself explicitly calls Anchin the “sweet refuge of my exile.” Consequently, **c. 1108–1112, perhaps around 1110**, is defensible, but an exact year remains unverified. <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="Sellner, Harald [VerfasserIn]; Eberhard Karls Universität Tübingen [Grad-verleihende Institution] [Editor]: Klöster zwischen Krise und correctio: monastische &quot;Reformen&quot; im Hochmittelalterlichen Flandern (Klöster als Innovationslabore: Studien und Texte, Tübingen, 2016)">[2]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="Sellner, Harald [VerfasserIn]; Eberhard Karls Universität Tübingen [Grad-verleihende Institution] [Editor]: Klöster zwischen Krise und correctio: monastische &quot;Reformen&quot; im Hochmittelalterlichen Flandern (Klöster als Innovationslabore: Studien und Texte, Tübingen, 2016)">[2]</a>

Odo had been elected at a council in Reims on **29 June 1105** and consecrated on 2 July. His claim to Cambrai was opposed by Walcher/Gaucher, supported by imperial interests, while the city, its castellan, the count of Flanders, the archbishop of Reims, and the rival episcopal parties pursued changing alliances. Odo briefly entered Cambrai after Henry IV’s death in August 1106, but Henry V’s arrival and the political settlement of 1107 again made his position precarious. His own explanation emphasizes ecclesiology rather than local faction: imperial reinvestiture with ring and staff would imply that spiritual office came from royal power. <a class="cite" href="#ref-3" title="Sellner, Harald [VerfasserIn]; Eberhard Karls Universität Tübingen [Grad-verleihende Institution] [Editor]: Klöster zwischen Krise und correctio: monastische &quot;Reformen&quot; im Hochmittelalterlichen Flandern (Klöster als Innovationslabore: Studien und Texte, Tübingen, 2016)">[3]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-3" title="Sellner, Harald [VerfasserIn]; Eberhard Karls Universität Tübingen [Grad-verleihende Institution] [Editor]: Klöster zwischen Krise und correctio: monastische &quot;Reformen&quot; im Hochmittelalterlichen Flandern (Klöster als Innovationslabore: Studien und Texte, Tübingen, 2016)">[3]</a>

This was the wider setting of the Investiture Contest. Henry V extracted the controversial investiture privilege from Paschal II in 1111; its repudiation in 1112 further complicated the position of bishops caught between imperial and papal obedience. Whether a separate 1112 deposition or excommunication of Odo occurred remains disputed: some narrative sources imply that he accepted imperial investiture, while Odo’s own prologue emphatically presents him as refusing it. The contradiction should not be silently harmonized. <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="Sellner, Harald [VerfasserIn]; Eberhard Karls Universität Tübingen [Grad-verleihende Institution] [Editor]: Klöster zwischen Krise und correctio: monastische &quot;Reformen&quot; im Hochmittelalterlichen Flandern (Klöster als Innovationslabore: Studien und Texte, Tübingen, 2016)">[2]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="Sellner, Harald [VerfasserIn]; Eberhard Karls Universität Tübingen [Grad-verleihende Institution] [Editor]: Klöster zwischen Krise und correctio: monastische &quot;Reformen&quot; im Hochmittelalterlichen Flandern (Klöster als Innovationslabore: Studien und Texte, Tübingen, 2016)">[2]</a>

The immediate audience was **Amandus, a monk of Anchin**, and probably the educated monastic circle around him. Amand repeatedly pressed Odo for a solution not adequately answering his particular doubts in existing patristic expositions. The learned, dialectical form suggests readers trained in monastic or cathedral-school disputation. The *Histoire littéraire de la France* (IX, reprinted at PL 160, cols. 1047–1048) identifies him with **Amand du Chastel (Amandus de Castello)**, then a simple monk of Anchin, soon afterwards its prior and later abbot of Marchiennes, the author of the notice on Odo’s last illness and death, and reports that Amand himself attributes the treatise to Odo; the identification rests on that eighteenth-century scholarship rather than on a statement in the text, and Douai MS 201 places Amand’s notice (item 11) immediately after the treatise. <a class="cite" href="#ref-4" title="Patrologiae cursus completus">[4]</a>

## Theological Significance

Odo begins with the apparent contradiction between the Church’s universal proclamation of pardon and Christ’s declaration that one sin will not be forgiven. He coordinates the three Synoptic passages—Matthew 12:31–32, Mark 3:28–30, and Luke 12:10—through an old Gospel-harmony system: *“secundo canone, capitulo Matthaei XII, Marci XXXIV, Lucae XLVII.”* “Second canon” refers to the **second Eusebian canon**, containing material common to Matthew, Mark, and Luke; the following numerals are Ammonian/Eusebian section numbers, not modern biblical chapters. This is valuable evidence that Odo’s exegesis was materially organized through canon tables as well as continuous Gospel manuscripts. <a class="cite" href="#ref-4" title="Patrologiae cursus completus">[4]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-4" title="Patrologiae cursus completus">[4]</a>

His central solution identifies blasphemy against the Spirit with the **“sin unto death” of 1 John 5:16**, understood as impenitence persevering until death. It is not an isolated utterance that magically exceeds divine mercy. An earlier act of blasphemy, malice, or impenitence can itself be forgiven if the sinner later repents. What cannot be forgiven is the terminal condition of refusing repentance, because repentance is the divinely appointed mode by which forgiveness is received. To say that God “cannot” forgive final impenitence therefore describes a moral and logical incompatibility—not a defect in omnipotence. <a class="cite" href="#ref-4" title="Patrologiae cursus completus">[4]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-4" title="Patrologiae cursus completus">[4]</a>

This leads Odo into characteristically dialectical territory. He distinguishes what God possesses or does necessarily from what it would be unfitting or contradictory for God to do. A human being can waive an injury without repentance because a private creditor can relinquish a claim; God’s relation to sin also involves perfectly ordered justice and the healing of the sinner. Odo then arranges the argument diagrammatically into four related states: **penitence and remission**, goods proceeding from mercy; **impenitence and irremission**, evils or penal conditions ordered by justice. The printed figure at PL 160, col. 1117 should be consulted in the scanned page, because plain-text transcriptions omit its spatial relationships. It resembles a logical *quaternio* or diagram of contraries, although calling it a formal Aristotelian “square of opposition” would require fuller manuscript comparison.

Odo’s conclusion is strikingly close to Augustine’s *Sermo 71*: Augustine also explains the unforgivable sin as the lasting resistance of an impenitent heart to the Spirit’s gift of forgiveness and ecclesial unity. Augustine says that resistance to forgiveness belongs to one who has *“duritiam cordis impoenitentis.”* Yet Odo states that the Augustinian book mentioned in the *Enchiridion* had never come into his hands. If that report is accepted, Odo represents an **independent reconstruction of an Augustinian solution**, formed from Scripture and general Augustinian theology rather than direct use of *Sermo 71*. (<a class="cite" href="#ref-5" title="Augustine: Sermon 71">[5]</a>; <a class="cite" href="#ref-17" title="English translation">[17]</a>). <a class="cite" href="#ref-5" title="Augustine: Sermon 71">[5]</a>

Bede’s interpretation, preserved in the later *Catena aurea*, similarly connects the sin with impenitence, while distinguishing malicious opposition from errors committed through ignorance. Gregory the Great is relevant to the wider medieval theology of repentance and the Spirit, but I did not locate a secure passage demonstrating Odo’s direct dependence on Gregory. <a class="cite" href="#ref-6" title="St. Thomas Aquinas: Catena Aurea - Gospel of Mark - Christian Classics Ethereal Library">[6]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-6" title="St. Thomas Aquinas: Catena Aurea - Gospel of Mark - Christian Classics Ethereal Library">[6]</a>

By the middle of the twelfth century, Peter Lombard’s *Sentences* II, distinction 43, offered a more elaborate classification: despair, presumption, impenitence, obstinacy, resistance to known truth, and envy of another’s grace. Odo’s account is simpler and more tightly organized around final impenitence, justice, mercy, and forgiveness. There is **no demonstrated direct borrowing** by Lombard; the comparison establishes doctrinal development, not literary dependence. Direct parallels with the school of Anselm of Laon and Hugh of Saint-Victor deserve further manuscript-level collation but were not securely verified in this research pass. <a class="cite" href="#ref-7" title="LombardPress Reader">[7]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-7" title="LombardPress Reader">[7]</a>

Odo’s position appears orthodox rather than heterodox. His language about things God “cannot” do could sound provocative when removed from context, but it serves the conventional claim that divine omnipotence does not include contradiction, injustice, or moral defect. No medieval condemnation or major reception controversy attached specifically to this treatise has been located. Its direct influence appears limited or at least undocumented; its importance lies chiefly in showing a school-trained logician applying Boethian habits of distinction and diagram to a pastoral and exegetical problem.

## Manuscript Tradition and Editions

At least **two manuscript witnesses** can be securely identified. **Douai, Bibliothèque Marceline Desbordes-Valmore, MS 201**, a twelfth-century parchment codex of 118 folios from Anchin Abbey, contains at fol. 112v (item 10) a *Tractatus domni Odonis, Cameracensis episcopi, quæ sit blasphemia Spiritus*, with the incipit *De blasphemia sancti Spiritus se conscripsisse libellum* and the desinit *Qui vult scire requirat apud illos* — exactly the limits of Migne’s text — followed (item 11) by Amand du Chastel’s notice on Odo. Its Anchin provenance makes it especially important for the work’s origin and early circulation. The catalogue specifies the opening folio but not a complete folio range. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="10&lt;sup&gt;o&lt;/sup&gt; (Fol. 112 v&lt;sup&gt;o&lt;/sup&gt;). « Tractatus domni Odonis, Cameracensis episcopi, quæ sit blasphemia Spiritus »">[1]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="10&lt;sup&gt;o&lt;/sup&gt; (Fol. 112 v&lt;sup&gt;o&lt;/sup&gt;). « Tractatus domni Odonis, Cameracensis episcopi, quæ sit blasphemia Spiritus »">[1]</a>

A second twelfth-century witness is **Cologne, Dombibliothek, Theol. 1531**, a parchment quarto of 135 leaves in two columns, formerly owned by St Pantaleon in Cologne, described by Paul Lehmann in 1938. It places Odo’s *De peccato originali* at pp. 93b–124b and *De blasphemia* at pp. 124b–130b, under the same title as the Anchin copy (*Tractatus domni Odonis Cameracensis episcopi que sit blasphemia spiritus*), followed by Odo’s *De adventu Christi* and, further on (pp. 267b–269a), by Augustine’s sermon on the same Gospel text (*Qui blasphemaverit in spiritum sanctum*, Sermo 71) — the sermon Odo says he had never seen. This pairing supports the relationship between the two works and demonstrates circulation beyond the Cambrai–Anchin region. <a class="cite" href="#ref-8" title="BAdW · 012762877">[8]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-8" title="BAdW · 012762877">[8]</a>

I did **not** verify a Cambrai manuscript containing this particular treatise. Cambrai catalogue records containing other works of Odo should not automatically be counted as witnesses of *De blasphemia*. Nor did I locate a digitized image of the relevant Douai or Cologne folios.

Migne’s text in **PL 160, cols. 1111–1118** is pre-critical and lacks a modern apparatus. Migne’s own heading for Odo’s *Opuscula sacra* (PL 160, col. 1053) states that they were first brought to light “for the most part” by the Jesuit Andreas Schott and are reprinted from the *Bibliotheca Patrum*, vol. XXI, p. 221 — the *Maxima Bibliotheca Veterum Patrum* (Lyon, 1677); the *Histoire littéraire de la France* IX, reprinted in the same volume (cols. 1045–1048), places *De blasphemia* in that collection immediately after the three other treatises and notes that Amand du Chastel attributes it to Odo. An earlier association of the text with Luc d’Achery’s *Spicilegium* was not supported: no Odo entry was found in the *Spicilegium* scans searched. Which manuscript Schott printed from is not stated in these sources; identifying it with the Anchin book now Douai MS 201 would be an inference, not an established fact. No modern critical edition in CCSL, CCCM, CSEL, or Sources Chrétiennes, and no complete modern translation, was located.

## Author Biography

Odo was born at **Orléans, probably around 1060**, although older reference works sometimes give approximately 1050. He was educated or taught for a time at Toul before being invited to direct the cathedral school of Tournai, generally dated to about **1087–1092**. Herman of Tournai depicts him as a master of international reputation, attracting students from France, Flanders, Normandy, Italy, Saxony, and Burgundy. The geographical catalogue is probably panegyrical, but it reflects a real memory of Tournai’s elevated intellectual standing under Odo. <a class="cite" href="#ref-10" title="few years before Abelard arrived in Paris, another teacher rose to prominence on the basis of his reputation. Odo of Orléans eminently directed the school of Tournai for five years (1086–92) before giving it up for the monastic life. After his schooling at Toul, the canons of St. Mary’s in Tournai appointed him a master on the basis of his reputation:">[10]</a>

Around 1092 Odo left the schools for the restored religious community of Saint-Martin of Tournai. The house subsequently adopted Benedictine observance, with close institutional ties to Anchin. By 1105 it had become a substantial monastic community. Odo’s election as bishop of Cambrai in June 1105 removed him from Saint-Martin, but he maintained enduring connections with his former monastery and with Anchin. <a class="cite" href="#ref-11" title="Sellner, Harald [VerfasserIn]; Eberhard Karls Universität Tübingen [Grad-verleihende Institution] [Editor]: Klöster zwischen Krise und correctio: monastische &quot;Reformen&quot; im Hochmittelalterlichen Flandern (Klöster als Innovationslabore: Studien und Texte, Tübingen, 2016)">[11]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-11" title="Sellner, Harald [VerfasserIn]; Eberhard Karls Universität Tübingen [Grad-verleihende Institution] [Editor]: Klöster zwischen Krise und correctio: monastische &quot;Reformen&quot; im Hochmittelalterlichen Flandern (Klöster als Innovationslabore: Studien und Texte, Tübingen, 2016)">[11]</a>

His episcopate was dominated by contested elections, rival bishops, the Investiture Contest, and shifting Flemish and imperial politics. He spent comparatively little of his episcopate securely established in Cambrai: at different times he resided at Saint-Martin, the castle of Inchy, and Anchin. After a short return to Cambrai in 1112, serious illness led him back to Anchin. Amandus de Castello reports that he relinquished the episcopate and had himself carried there. Odo died at Anchin on **19 June 1113** (Sellner; Le Glay’s notice reprinted at PL 160, col. 1044A gives *XII Kalendas Julii*, 20 June — the day is not resolved here) and was buried at the abbey, according to the same notice under a white marble slab carved with his image. <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="Sellner, Harald [VerfasserIn]; Eberhard Karls Universität Tübingen [Grad-verleihende Institution] [Editor]: Klöster zwischen Krise und correctio: monastische &quot;Reformen&quot; im Hochmittelalterlichen Flandern (Klöster als Innovationslabore: Studien und Texte, Tübingen, 2016)">[2]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="Sellner, Harald [VerfasserIn]; Eberhard Karls Universität Tübingen [Grad-verleihende Institution] [Editor]: Klöster zwischen Krise und correctio: monastische &quot;Reformen&quot; im Hochmittelalterlichen Flandern (Klöster als Innovationslabore: Studien und Texte, Tübingen, 2016)">[2]</a>

Odo’s surviving works include *De peccato originali*, *Disputatio contra Judaeum Leonem*, *Expositio in canonem missae*, *De blasphemia in Spiritum sanctum*, and a work on Gospel canons; several sermons or homilies have also been attributed to him with varying confidence. Resnick’s 1994 volume translates *De peccato originali* and the *Disputatio*, but not *De blasphemia*. <a class="cite" href="#ref-12" title="On Original Sin ; And, A Disputation with the Jew, Leo, Concerning the ... - Odo (of Tournai) - Google Books">[12]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-12" title="On Original Sin ; And, A Disputation with the Jew, Leo, Concerning the ... - Odo (of Tournai) - Google Books">[12]</a>

Philosophically, Odo is remembered as an advocate of realism concerning universals. Herman, writing several decades later, contrasts Raimbert of Lille’s dialectic *in voce* with Odo’s teaching *in re*, “in the manner of Boethius and the ancient doctors.” Modern historians treat that passage cautiously: it is retrospective and polemical, not a neutral transcript of an eleventh-century debate. Nevertheless, *De peccato originali* confirms that Odo employed a strong account of common human substance to explain inherited sin. *De blasphemia* reveals the same intellectual temperament in miniature—precise distinctions, argument through opposites, and the visual organization of theological relations. <a class="cite" href="#ref-13" title="Volume2.dvi">[13]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-13" title="Volume2.dvi">[13]</a>
