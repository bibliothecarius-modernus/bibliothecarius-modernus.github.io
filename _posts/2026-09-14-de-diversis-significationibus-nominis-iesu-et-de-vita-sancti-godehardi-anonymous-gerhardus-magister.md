## Historical Context

### Authorship and attribution

**Text A, *De diversis significationibus nominis Iesu*, is anonymous.** The Wolfenbüttel catalogue supplies no author and describes it by title, incipit, and content. Its use of Bernard of Clairvaux’s *Sermones super Cantica* 15.6 demonstrates dependence on a source composed in the twelfth century, but it does not identify the compiler. The compiler’s gender, office, and institutional affiliation cannot be recovered from the surviving copy. In particular, the fact that the booklet was produced at a convent of Cistercian women does not establish that a nun composed the text.

**Text B is attributed only by its rubric, *Sermo magistri Gerhardi*.** Bertram Lesser’s catalogue treats “Gerhardus magister” as unidentified. Otto von Heinemann’s nineteenth-century index assigned the sermon to Gerard Zerbolt of Zutphen, but a letter from Westenhoff of Ghent dated 19 May 1890 rejected that identification and cautiously proposed a cleric of the Diocese of Hildesheim. The safest modern formulation is therefore: **an otherwise unidentified Magister Gerhardus, probably connected with Hildesheim if the sermon’s internal community-language is authorial**. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="https://diglib.hab.de/?db=mss&id=Cod.+Guelf.+602+Helmst.&list=mssdoku">[1]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="https://diglib.hab.de/?db=mss&id=Cod.+Guelf.+602+Helmst.&list=mssdoku">[1]</a>

### Date and place

What can be dated securely is the **copy**, not necessarily each composition. Part I of Cod. Guelf. 602 Helmst. is a twenty-leaf parchment booklet assigned palaeographically to the **second half of the thirteenth century**. It measures 21 × 14 cm, has 28 lines per page, and was written in one regular textualis hand with rubrication and red lombards. <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="https://diglib.hab.de/?catalog=Lesser&db=mss&id=602-helmst&list=ms">[2]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="https://diglib.hab.de/?catalog=Lesser&db=mss&id=602-helmst&list=ms">[2]</a>

Text A must postdate Bernard’s sermon, but no narrower date of composition has been established. Text B must postdate Godehard’s death and cult; its surviving copy is thirteenth-century, but it could preserve an earlier sermon. Its conclusion—*qui ex nobis natus, in nobis etiam conversatus, nobis postmodum est prelatus*—presents Godehard as born among “us,” living among “us,” and later becoming “our” prelate. This strongly suggests an original Hildesheim institutional audience, though it does not identify a particular church or monastery.

The booklet itself was copied at Wöltingerode. The house had been confirmed by Bishop Adelog of Hildesheim in 1174; archival evidence says that its initial Benedictine community had been replaced by Cistercian women by the royal protection granted in 1188. <a class="cite" href="#ref-3" title="https://www.arcinsys.niedersachsen.de/arcinsys/showFondsDetails?fondsId=1000">[3]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-3" title="https://www.arcinsys.niedersachsen.de/arcinsys/showFondsDetails?fondsId=1000">[3]</a>

### Historical setting and audience

The texts belong to the religious culture of the Hildesheim–Goslar region: a landscape of episcopal churches, monasteries, women’s convents, schools, and local saints’ cults within the Holy Roman Empire. Wöltingerode’s surviving books show sustained engagement with Latin devotional, monastic, homiletic, and allegedly patristic literature. Later scholarship has characterized its reconstructed late-medieval collection as one of the more extensive surviving convent libraries of northern Germany, although most of that collection is fifteenth-century. <a class="cite" href="#ref-4" title="https://www.hab.de/en/die-buchbestaende-von-woeltingerode/">[4]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-4" title="https://www.hab.de/en/die-buchbestaende-von-woeltingerode/">[4]</a>

The immediate audience of Part I was probably the Wöltingerode community and its clerical assistants. Its sequence—from the *Jubilus* on Jesus’ name through moral instruction, episcopal hagiography, authoritative extracts, Easter preaching, and Mary Magdalene—would support devotional reading, instruction, sermon preparation, and communal edification. Text A fits affective meditation on Jesus; Text B offers a model of obedient formation and responsible government. The Wöltingerode witness is direct evidence that a Godehard sermon was available in **one Cistercian women’s house** by the later thirteenth century. It is not enough by itself to establish widespread circulation among medieval women’s houses.

The individual parts were combined in their present binding at Wöltingerode in the late fifteenth century. The volume was transferred to the Wolfenbüttel court library on **14 March 1572** and placed in the Helmstedt university library in 1618. <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="https://diglib.hab.de/?catalog=Lesser&db=mss&id=602-helmst&list=ms">[2]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="https://diglib.hab.de/?catalog=Lesser&db=mss&id=602-helmst&list=ms">[2]</a>

## Theological Significance

### Text A: the meanings and effects of Jesus’ name

Text A is not a scholastic quaestio defining different lexical meanings. It is an accumulative, litany-like work in which names, metaphors, sensory images, and spiritual effects are placed one after another. Jesus is described as joy for the sorrowful, eternity for the living, health for the sick, abundance for the needy, and satisfaction for the hungry. The governing theological idea is that the name “Jesus” both **signifies salvation** and becomes an instrument of recollection, consolation, purification, illumination, and petition.

Its best-attested literary foundation is Bernard of Clairvaux’s *Sermones super Cantica* 15.6: Jesus is “honey in the mouth, music in the ear, jubilation in the heart,” and writing has no savour unless Jesus is read there. The passage joins taste, hearing, inward emotion, reading, and memory. Text A expands that compact Bernardine meditation into a catalogue suitable for slow reading or oral recitation. <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="https://diglib.hab.de/?catalog=Lesser&db=mss&id=602-helmst&list=ms">[2]</a>; an English rendering is also cited in the <a class="cite" href="#ref-9" title="Vatican’s Bernard audience">[9]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="https://diglib.hab.de/?catalog=Lesser&db=mss&id=602-helmst&list=ms">[2]</a>

The principal biblical image identified in the text is Song of Songs 1:2 in the Vulgate numbering, *oleum effusum nomen tuum*: “your name is oil poured out.” Oil can nourish, heal, illuminate, consecrate, and produce fragrance, allowing the compiler to move naturally from scriptural exegesis to lists of the name’s spiritual operations. Matthew 1:21—“you shall call his name Jesus, for he shall save his people”—and Philippians 2:9–11 form the broader biblical theology of the saving and exalted name, but they should not be presented as explicit quotations unless confirmed in the full edition.

The text belongs to the same broad devotional environment as the *Dulcis Iesu memoria* tradition copied immediately before it. Modern scholarship treats that hymn as a twelfth-century work attributed to Bernard only later. Denis Renevey has shown that the hymn’s repeated invocations and the measures adopted at the Second Council of Lyon in 1274 contributed to increasingly visible devotion to the Name during the thirteenth century. This supplies historical context, not proof that the council directly caused the copying of Text A. <a class="cite" href="#ref-5" title="https://academic.oup.com/book/44010/chapter-abstract/371860089">[5]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-5" title="https://academic.oup.com/book/44010/chapter-abstract/371860089">[5]</a>

### Text B: formation, office, and pastoral government

Text B presents sanctity through three social and institutional conditions: **subject, companion, and prelate**—*subiectus, socius, prelatus*. Godehard first learns obedience, then lives peaceably within a monastic community, and finally governs others. The sermon is therefore less a continuous biography than a moralized account of how legitimate authority should be formed.

Psalm 44:5 provides its lemma: *Specie tua et pulchritudine tua intende, prospere procede et regna*. The sermon uses Godehard’s stages of life to explain movement from disciplined attention through progress to rule. Its strongest polemic is directed against those who pass *de cunis ad cathedram*, “from the cradle to the chair,” or *de taxillis ad prelaturam*, “from dice to prelacy.” Authority without formation produces rulers who strike with “scorpions,” echoing Rehoboam in 3 Kings 12:11.

The sermon opposes such domination with Christ’s pastoral pattern. Matthew 23:4 condemns leaders who place heavy burdens on others; Matthew 11:30 presents Christ’s yoke as sweet and his burden light. John 21’s threefold *pasce* makes feeding rather than coercion the defining work of a prelate. First Peter 5:4 supplies the closing promise of the unfading pastoral crown. The Persius quotation *non tressis agaso* (5.76) adds a satirical classical voice to the sermon’s attack on unqualified advancement. A targeted search did not locate a specialist study of this particular Persius line in medieval preaching, so its frequency or conventionality should not be overstated.

### Patristic context, controversy, and influence

Neither text mounts a demonstrable argument from Augustine, Jerome, or Gregory the Great. Text A’s named authority is Bernard, a medieval monastic theologian rather than a Father of the Church. The surrounding booklet, however, contains works attributed—sometimes incorrectly—to Basil, Augustine, Bernard, Origen, and others. This illustrates how patristic and pseudo-patristic authority was received through miscellanies and florilegia rather than through modern critical distinctions.

No heterodox teaching is evident. The difficulties are bibliographical: uncertain authorship, incomplete transmission, pseudo-attribution in neighboring texts, and possible dependence on unidentified devotional sources. Nor is there evidence that either short work independently shaped later Christian thought. Text A participates in a larger Name-of-Jesus tradition; Text B witnesses local Godehard devotion and episcopal ethics. Participation in a tradition must not be confused with demonstrable influence upon it.

## Manuscript Tradition and Editions

Both complete texts are presently documented in **Wolfenbüttel, Herzog August Bibliothek, Cod. Guelf. 602 Helmst., Part I**. Text A occupies ff. 2v–3v. The HAB catalogue says that it has so far been documented only here “in this form,” while also recognizing that its Bernardine opening and shorter related treatments circulated elsewhere. Thus the defensible claim is: **no modern edition or machine-readable transcription of the complete Text A was located; in this form it is currently known from Cod. Guelf. 602 Helmst.** It must not be described as wholly unknown elsewhere, since versions of its opening litany are reported in Paris, BnF lat. 3760, in the variant discussed by Martha Bayless, and in Heinrich von Langenstein’s *Orationes de quinque litteris nominis Ihesu*.

Text B occupies ff. 16v–17v (lines 16v.10–17v.19). The catalogue’s heading displays “16–17v”; the rubric and incipit stand on f. 16v. Lesser describes it as unprinted and not otherwise identified. The sermon’s opening relative clause may indicate that its original beginning has been lost, possibly through scribal omission. <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="https://diglib.hab.de/?catalog=Lesser&db=mss&id=602-helmst&list=ms">[2]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="https://diglib.hab.de/?catalog=Lesser&db=mss&id=602-helmst&list=ms">[2]</a>

There is **no Migne PL text** for either item and no CSEL, CCSL, CCCM, or *Sources chrétiennes* edition was located. PL references in the manuscript catalogue concern neighboring works, notably the pseudo-Basilian *Admonitio*, not these two texts. No published English, French, or German translation of either complete text was located as of 14 September 2026. The present Bibliothecarius Modernus transcription should therefore be described as a new manuscript-based editorial project, not as a revision of Migne.

## Author Biography

A conventional author biography cannot responsibly be supplied for either work.

The compiler of Text A is wholly anonymous. The text shows knowledge of Bernard’s *Sermones super Cantica* and of biblical language associated with the Name of Jesus. Its construction suggests someone experienced in assembling devotional authorities, parallel phrases, and spiritual effects. That profile is compatible with a monk, nun, canon, priest, school-trained cleric, or later copyist-compiler; it is not enough to choose among them. No birth, education, office, other works, or date of death is known. The Wöltingerode provenance belongs securely to the surviving copy but should not automatically be transferred to the original act of composition.

The author named in Text B is likewise known only from the rubric *Sermo magistri Gerhardi*. “Magister” indicates recognized learning or status, but it does not by itself prove that Gerhardus held a university degree. His sermon displays competence in biblical exegesis, monastic ideals, the ethics of prelacy, hagiographical construction, and classical quotation. The final first-person plural suggests that he spoke or wrote for a Hildesheim community that regarded Godehard as one of its own. This is the principal internal evidence for his institutional background.

Heinemann indexed the work under Gerard Zerbolt of Zutphen. Westenhoff’s 1890 letter rejected that attribution and proposed, cautiously, an otherwise unknown Hildesheim diocesan cleric. Lesser’s current catalogue adopts the more conservative position: the rubric’s author cannot be identified more closely. R. Th. M. van Dijk’s *Prolegomena ad Gerardi Magni opera omnia* cites Cod. Guelf. 602 Helmst. on p. 431, according to the HAB bibliographical database, but the wording and purpose of that citation have not been independently verified. It should therefore not be treated as a renewed attribution to Gerard Zerbolt, Geert Grote, or another known Gerard. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="https://diglib.hab.de/?db=mss&id=Cod.+Guelf.+602+Helmst.&list=mssdoku">[1]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="https://diglib.hab.de/?db=mss&id=Cod.+Guelf.+602+Helmst.&list=mssdoku">[1]</a>

The appropriate legacy statement is correspondingly modest. Anonymous and Gerhardus are not known literary figures with documented schools or followers. Their importance lies in what the manuscript preserves: a regional form of Name-of-Jesus devotion and a locally voiced sermon on the formation and duties of a Hildesheim bishop.

For comparison, Godehard’s principal medieval biographer was Wolfhere of Hildesheim. The *Geschichtsquellen* database dates Wolfhere’s *Vita prior* shortly after 1035 and the *Vita posterior* to approximately 1054–1061. These vitae, rather than Gerhardus’s sermon, are the principal narrative sources for Godehard’s life. <a class="cite" href="#ref-6" title="https://geschichtsquellen.de/werk/4777?s=en">[6]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-6" title="https://geschichtsquellen.de/werk/4777?s=en">[6]</a>
