  <p>These twenty-six short Latin sermons fill the opening booklet of Houghton Library MS Lat 184, ff. 1r–16v, a German manuscript copied between 1150 and 1175. The manuscript gives them no title and names no author. Most of the sermons run to a few hundred words, open with Fratres karissimi, and close with a short formula such as Quod ipse prestet. They read as working models for a preacher addressing ordinary congregations: feasts of Mary and the saints, Lent, the dedication of a church, a funeral, Sunday observance, the Rogation days, and several pieces headed simply alius, “another,” that could serve on any occasion.</p>
  <p>Several passages stand out. One sermon warns: “We see the Roman Empire being destroyed. And beyond that, human laws have utterly failed.” Another speaks directly to the poor: “You who are poor, you who lack the possessions of this world: God will comfort you.” A sermon for the “Common Week” explains that alms are shared after the harvest “in memory of the living and the dead”; the calendar historian Hermann Grotefend records a Gemeine Woche after Michaelmas with a commemoration of the dead in northern Germany, which suggests, though it does not prove, where the booklet was used. A sermon addressed to priests is unusually blunt: “We consume the goods of the people, but we do not labor to win their souls for God,” for the bishop does not say “Receive riches and use them however you want,” but “Receive the care of souls, for which you will have to give an account on the day of judgment.”</p>
  <p>The booklet has lost leaves. The first sermon survives only in its closing lines, a leaf is missing after f. 1, the join between ff. 4v and 5r cannot be settled, and the last sermon breaks off at the foot of f. 16v. The library record mentions one sermon attributed to Hugh of St Victor in this section; no such attribution was located within these folios, and the sermons are presented here as anonymous.</p>
  <p>I have not located a previous edition, complete transcription, or English translation of these sermons. Schneyer’s Repertorium, Mirabile, and In Principio were not consulted. The Latin published here was transcribed directly from the manuscript images, independently reread, and reconciled against the images. Readings that remain uncertain, mostly expansions of abbreviations, are listed with their folio and line, and five that affect the sense are marked in the text.</p>

Manuscript imagery used in the edition is credited to the Houghton Library under Harvard Library’s policy on digital reproductions of public-domain works; no endorsement is implied.</p>

## Historical Context

### Authorship

“Anonymous” is the correct **cataloguing status**, not a personal name or a confirmed authorial identity. The manuscript gives no collective title and no authorial rubric for ff. 1r–16v. The current Houghton catalogue describes this section as unidentified sermons, with one attribution to Hugh of St Victor; however, the 2026 reconciled transcription did not locate that attribution within these folios. It is therefore not attached to any sermon in this edition. Nor should the presence of works by Geoffrey Babion, Radulfus de Turbine, Hugh, and patristic authors elsewhere in the composite codex be used to attribute the opening booklet. The safest description is **anonymous compilation, possibly drawing on several authors or sermon traditions**. (ER-1–2; <a class="cite" href="#ref-27" title="id.lib.harvard.edu">[27]</a>; <a class="cite" href="#ref-31" title="IIIF manifest">[31]</a>).

### Date and provenance

The secure date is the date of the manuscript witness: Houghton’s current record assigns the codex to **Germany, between 1150 and 1175**. That provides a terminus ante quem for the booklet in its present form, not a secure composition date for every component sermon. Some pieces may have been copied from substantially older exemplars. (ER-3).

The strongest internal regional pointer is the sermon for the *communis ebdomada*. Grotefend defines *Gemeine Woche / hebdomada communis* as usually the full week after Michaelmas and cites prayers for both living and dead, together with a Verden calendar’s *Memoria omnium animarum*. The sermon’s post-harvest almsgiving “in memory of the living and the dead” corresponds closely to that usage. This supports an inference of **northern or north-central German use**, but it cannot establish a particular diocese or scriptorium. (ER-4; Grotefend, <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="Grotefend | Glossar">[2]</a>). <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="Grotefend | Glossar">[2]</a>

### Historical setting and intended audience

The collection belongs to the pastoral world that also produced Honorius Augustodunensis’s early-twelfth-century *Speculum ecclesiae* and the developing German sermon complexes known as the *Speculum ecclesiae deutsch*, the Leipzig and Hoffmann collections, the *Predigtbuch Priester Konrads*, and the St Paul sermons. These collections were not normally fixed literary works in the modern sense: preachers excerpted, shortened, expanded, translated, and recombined material for particular occasions. Schiewer describes early German preaching as a process of compilation and adaptation, while Honorius explicitly directs that a scriptural verse be pronounced in Latin and then explained *patria lingua*. (ER-5–7; Schiewer, pp. 39, 47–53, <a class="cite" href="#ref-3" title="2.5. Die Struktur — 47">[3]</a>; Honorius, *Speculum ecclesiae*, PL 172). <a class="cite" href="#ref-3" title="2.5. Die Struktur — 47">[3]</a>

The palaeographical window overlaps Frederick Barbarossa’s reign and, in its later portion, the Alexandrine schism of 1159–1177. Prayer for pope and emperor therefore had immediate resonance, although it was also a conventional Christian duty and does not identify the compiler’s political allegiance. The primary user was probably a priest or other preacher: one sermon says “we are your priests,” while another addresses priests directly about their episcopally imposed *cura animarum*. The expected hearers were predominantly laypeople—including the poor, landholders, families, mourners, and parish communities—who would normally have heard the Latin material translated or freely paraphrased in the vernacular. (ER-8–10). <a class="cite" href="#ref-4" title="Internet History Sourcebooks: Medieval Sourcebook">[4]</a>

## Theological Significance

The collection’s theology is practical rather than speculative. Its governing themes are repentance, almsgiving, preparation for death, the heavenly homeland, observance of feast days and Sunday, the intercession of Mary and the saints, social responsibility, and pastoral accountability. Salvation is presented as a communal and liturgical project: people gather, hear Scripture, give alms, pray for living and dead, honour sacred time and space, and submit their conduct to judgment. The sermons to the poor and the estates-oriented pieces do not propose social revolution; they insist instead that poverty, wealth, priesthood, judicial power, and household life each carry moral obligations. The admonition to priests is especially sharp: “We eat the goods of the people and do not labour,” and bishops give priests not riches but “the care of souls.” This belongs to the long Gregorian tradition in which a preacher must first examine and correct himself before correcting others. (ER-11; Gregory, *Pastoral Rule* III–IV, <a class="cite" href="#ref-5" title="Internet History Sourcebooks: Medieval Sourcebook">[5]</a>). <a class="cite" href="#ref-5" title="Internet History Sourcebooks: Medieval Sourcebook">[5]</a>

The booklet is patristic in vocabulary and method, but direct dependence on Augustine, Jerome, Gregory, Caesarius, Bede, or another Father has not yet been demonstrated sermon by sermon. Its plain exhortation, repeated *Fratres karissimi*, concern for comprehensibility, and moral summaries resemble the late-antique popular preaching associated particularly with Caesarius of Arles. Its dedication sermons belong to a tradition in which the Zacchaeus pericope, Luke 19:1–10, was a standard dedication Gospel: A dedication homily on Zacchaeus is also transmitted among the homilies printed under Bede’s name (PL 94, *Homiliae subditiae* LXVI). The funeral sermon’s Ecclesiastes 7:2–3 theme—“better to go to the house of mourning”—turns the funeral into instruction for the living. (ER-12–13). <a class="cite" href="#ref-6" title="&quot;These Are Not Men&quot;: Sex and Drink in the Sermons of Caesarius of Arles">[6]</a>

Several episodes preserve identifiable inherited topoi. The Rogation sermon’s Mamertus narrative belongs to the Gallic tradition witnessed by Sidonius and Avitus and still repeated by twelfth-century liturgists such as Rupert of Deutz and Sicard of Cremona. The Sunday sermon’s list of sacred events—creation, Christ’s resurrection, the gift of manna—is found in Isidore’s *De ecclesiasticis officiis* (I.25, PL 83, col. 761). Thus these passages are better understood as transmission of ancient homiletic material than as original inventions by the Houghton compiler. (ER-14–15). <a class="cite" href="#ref-7" title="Sidonius Apollinaris, Letters. Tr. O.M. Dalton (1915) vol. 2. pp. 95-137 ; Book VII">[7]</a>

Biblical foundations include Matthew 18:3 for the Michael sermon; Luke 19 for church dedication; Ecclesiastes 7 for funerals; 1 Timothy 2:1–4 for prayer for rulers; and the broader Pauline apocalyptic tradition behind “we see the Roman Empire being destroyed.” Second Thessalonians 2 was traditionally interpreted as making the Roman Empire or emperor the force restraining Antichrist. Adso’s influential tenth-century synthesis likewise made the Empire’s dissolution a precursor of Antichrist. The Houghton sentence is therefore first an **eschatological commonplace**. A reference to the conflicts of the Barbarossa decades is possible, but remains an inference, not a historical identification. (ER-16; <a class="cite" href="#ref-8" title="2 Thessalonians, CHAPTER 2 | USCCB">[8]</a>). <a class="cite" href="#ref-8" title="2 Thessalonians, CHAPTER 2 | USCCB">[8]</a>

No heterodox position has been identified. Even the phrase *post liberi arbitrii amissionem* should not, in isolation, be read as a novel denial of human agency: a related formulation occurs in Martin of León and probably descends from an older shared source. Likewise, the coexistence of prayers for pope and emperor suggests ordinary Christian intercession for both powers, not a worked-out political theory. There is currently **no demonstrable reception history** for this particular booklet: its importance lies in documenting pastoral practice, not in a proven influence on later theology. (ER-17–18).

## Manuscript Tradition and Editions

The only witness presently located for this precise twenty-six-piece sequence is **Cambridge, Massachusetts, Houghton Library, MS Lat 184, ff. 1r–16v**. This is not a claim of uniqueness: no complete search has yet been made in Schneyer’s anonymous incipits, Mirabile, or the subscription database *In Principio*. Those resources are essential because early sermon traditions often survive as rearranged excerpts rather than identical collections. (ER-19; <a class="cite" href="#ref-9" title="Brepols - Series - In Principio - online">[9]</a>). <a class="cite" href="#ref-9" title="Brepols - Series - In Principio - online">[9]</a>

**Patrologia Latina:** there is no PL text of this collection. PL 172 contains Honorius’s *Speculum ecclesiae*, a relevant comparison but not this work. PL 208 contains Martin of León and the parallel free-will phrase, but that does not make Martin the author or direct source. No CSEL, CCSL, Sources Chrétiennes, modern critical edition, or published translation has been located. No edition or translation has been located.

The 2026 Bibliothecarius transcription identifies twenty-six pieces, including fragments, where the library record counts twenty-four sermons. It is reconciled from two independent readings but is not curator-verified; approximately 1.4 percent of words retain uncertainty marks, mainly in abbreviation expansions. Major textual problems include a missing opening leaf, a lost leaf after f. 1, an uncertain join at ff. 4v–5r, and the incomplete ending at f. 16v. These losses prevent certainty about the first sermons’ titles and about whether the f. 5r commendation was independent or the conclusion of the preceding sermon. (ER-20–22).

## Author Biography

No conventional author biography can responsibly be written. There is no recoverable name, birthplace, education, ecclesiastical appointment, or date of death, and no securely attributable corpus beyond the material assembled in this booklet. Medieval sermon books commonly conflate several roles: an original preacher, a writer who produced a Latin model, an excerptor, a compiler who arranged the sequence, and the scribe who copied the surviving witness. Any of these roles may have been performed by different people.

A limited **functional profile** can nevertheless be inferred. The compiler was almost certainly clerically educated. He—or the sources he assembled—knew the Vulgate, the liturgical calendar, saints’ feasts, dedication and funeral rites, Rogation lore, prayers for ecclesiastical and secular rulers, and conventional patristic explanations of Sunday. The sequence’s short pieces, repeated addresses to *fratres karissimi*, adaptable rubrics such as *in quocumque festo* and *quando volueris*, and reusable conclusions are consistent with a working preacher’s book rather than an authorially polished literary cycle. This is an editorial inference from form and contents, not evidence that the compiler was necessarily the parish priest who used the codex. (ER-23).

The admonition to priests suggests close familiarity with episcopal oversight and the burdens of the *cura animarum*. The direct address to the poor and the criticism of those secure in “great castles” imply experience—or at least inherited sermon material—concerned with socially mixed congregations. The *communis ebdomada* sermon indicates that the compiler or a user adapted preaching to a regional post-harvest observance.

There is no basis for naming Hugh of St Victor as the author: the catalogue’s Hugh attribution has not been located within ff. 1r–16v, and the codex contains acknowledged Hugh material in later sections. The proper “legacy” of the anonymous compiler is consequently documentary: the booklet preserves evidence for how a twelfth-century German cleric could assemble patristic, liturgical, eschatological, social, and locally useful material into short models suitable for vernacular preaching. It does not preserve an identifiable literary career. (ER-2, ER-23).
