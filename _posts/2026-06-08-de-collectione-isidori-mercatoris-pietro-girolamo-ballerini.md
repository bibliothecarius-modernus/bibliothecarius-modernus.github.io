  An 18th-century critical dissertation examining the authenticity and historical development of the Pseudo-Isidorian Decretals, the influential collection of forged papal and conciliar documents that shaped medieval canon law for centuries before being exposed as fraudulent.
</p>

## Historical Context

**Authorship:** The treatise *De collectione Isidori Mercatoris* is unequivocally the work of the brothers **Pietro Ballerini** (1698–1769) and **Girolamo Ballerini** (1701–1781) ([www.newadvent.org](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02223b.htm#:~:text=Celebrated%20theologians%20%20and%20canonists%2C,9%2C%20also%20of%20Verona%2C%20and)) ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Gemeinsam%20publizierten%20die%20beiden%20Br%C3%BCder,hoher%20Qualit%C3%A4t%20gewesen%20sei%2C%20dass)). Both were 18th-century Italian Catholic scholars from Verona, renowned as theologians and canon lawyers. There is no dispute about their authorship – they jointly wrote and published this dissertation under their own names as part of their critical edition of **Pope Leo I’s works** ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Gemeinsam%20publizierten%20die%20beiden%20Br%C3%BCder,hoher%20Qualit%C3%A4t%20gewesen%20sei%2C%20dass)). The Ballerinis often collaborated so closely that their contributions are inseparable, and they published under both names. Modern scholarship unanimously attributes the work to them, with no hint of pseudonymity.

**Date of Composition:** The dissertation was researched and composed in the late 1740s and early 1750s and published in **1757** as part of the third volume (Appendix) of the Ballerini edition of *Leo the Great* ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Gemeinsam%20publizierten%20die%20beiden%20Br%C3%BCder,hoher%20Qualit%C3%A4t%20gewesen%20sei%2C%20dass)). Pope **Benedict XIV** commissioned Pietro Ballerini in **1748** to prepare a new edition of Leo’s writings to **supersede Pasquier Quesnel’s 1675 edition**, which had been tainted by Jansenist and Gallican ideas ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Republik%20Venedig%20%20damit%20beauftragt%2C,den%20Papst%20in%20kirchenrechtlichen%20und)) ([www.encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ballerini-pietro-and-girolamo#:~:text=1740%E2%80%9341,a%20history%20of%20probabilism%2C%20Saggio)). Pietro spent about nine years in research, with privileged access to libraries in Rome, before the completed edition was printed in Venice from **1753 to 1757** ([www.catholicity.com](https://www.catholicity.com/encyclopedia/b/ballerini%2Cgirolamo_and_pietro.html#:~:text=brothers%20famous%20throughout%20Italy%2C%20and,defective%20one%20published%20by%20Quesnel)) ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Gemeinsam%20publizierten%20die%20beiden%20Br%C3%BCder,hoher%20Qualit%C3%A4t%20gewesen%20sei%2C%20dass)). The *De collectione Isidori Mercatoris* forms chapter VI of Part III of a larger treatise (*Disquisitiones*), which appeared in the final volume in 1757. Its arguments and evidence were current as of the mid-18th century, reflecting the state of scholarship then.

**Provenance:** The work was largely **researched in Rome** under papal patronage. Pietro Ballerini resided in Rome during the project (starting 1748) to utilize the Vatican and other collections ([www.catholicity.com](https://www.catholicity.com/encyclopedia/b/ballerini%2Cgirolamo_and_pietro.html#:~:text=Aquileia,defective%20one%20published%20by%20Quesnel)), although the brothers likely corresponded and collaborated with each other from Verona. The final publication was issued in **Verona/Venice**, within the Republic of Venice, where the Ballerinis enjoyed support from local ecclesiastical and civil authorities ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=studierten%20am%20%C3%B6rtlichen%20Jesuitenkolleg%20,den%20Papst%20in%20kirchenrechtlichen%20und)). Notably, Pietro had been appointed by the Venetian Senate to represent Venice in a dispute with the Patriarchate of Aquileia, which brought him to Rome and into Benedict XIV’s favor ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=studierten%20am%20%C3%B6rtlichen%20Jesuitenkolleg%20,den%20Papst%20in%20kirchenrechtlichen%20und)). Thus, the dissertation emerged from a unique collaboration: researched in the scholarly climate of Rome but published in the Venetian milieu that fostered critical Catholic scholarship.

**Historical Setting:** The dissertation was written in the **Enlightenment-era Catholic world** amid intense religious and intellectual currents. In the early 18th century the Catholic Church was dealing with the fallout of **Jansenism** – Pasquier **Quesnel**, a prominent Jansenist, had edited Leo’s works with a Gallican slant and was later condemned (the papal bull *Unigenitus* in 1713 targeted Quesnel’s teachings). The Ballerini project was a direct response to this: Benedict XIV (a scholar-pope) wanted an edition free of Jansenist bias, and the Ballerinis’ work specifically answered and **refuted Quesnel’s interpretations** ([www.catholicity.com](https://www.catholicity.com/encyclopedia/b/ballerini%2Cgirolamo_and_pietro.html#:~:text=Aquileia,defective%20one%20published%20by%20Quesnel)) ([www.encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ballerini-pietro-and-girolamo#:~:text=1740%E2%80%9341,a%20history%20of%20probabilism%2C%20Saggio)). Politically, the Catholic Church was affirming papal authority against challenges: Gallicanism in France and Febronianism in the Holy Roman Empire questioned Rome’s primacy. Culturally, critical scholarship was on the rise – even Catholic academics were applying historical criticism to Church documents. The Ballerini brothers operated at this intersection of **church tradition and enlightened critical method**.

**Intended Audience:** The dissertation was aimed at an erudite audience of **church historians, canon lawyers, and Catholic authorities**. It appeared as part of a learned edition of a Church Father’s works, so its primary readership would be scholars and clergy interested in canon law and patristics. In particular, it addressed Catholic theologians and officials concerned with the authenticity of legal texts – Benedict XIV himself was an intended consumer of their findings ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Republik%20Venedig%20%20damit%20beauftragt%2C,den%20Papst%20in%20kirchenrechtlichen%20und)). By exposing the *False Decretals*, the Ballerinis were also implicitly dialoguing with Protestant scholars who had long questioned those texts. Indeed, they cite and build upon earlier Protestant research (e.g. the **Centuriators of Magdeburg** and David **Blondel**) ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Hinsichtlich%20der%20Pseudoisidorischen%20F%C3%A4lschungen%20,Zugleich%20suchten)). Thus, while the work served the Church’s need to counter Quesnel and uphold sound doctrine, it also engaged the wider 17th–18th century scholarly debate about forged documents. In tone, the dissertation sought to persuade Catholic readers of the falsity of Pseudo-Isidore **without undermining Catholic doctrine** – a delicate balance meant for educated readers, not the general faithful. Ultimately, its audience was the community of scholars and churchmen dedicated to purifying and understanding the historical sources of canon law.

## Theological Significance

**Core Themes:** The Ballerinis’ dissertation is a pioneering exercise in **historical criticism and forgery detection** within ecclesiastical studies. Its central argument is that the **“Isidorus Mercator” collection (Pseudo-Isidorean Decretals)** is a medieval forgery rather than an authentic set of early papal letters. The brothers marshal extensive evidence – textual, historical, and linguistic – to demonstrate anachronisms and inconsistencies in the False Decretals. They notably add new proofs to those first noted by Protestant critics, rather than attempting to defend the documents (a stance that set them apart from some earlier Catholic apologists) ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Hinsichtlich%20der%20Pseudoisidorischen%20F%C3%A4lschungen%20,Zugleich%20suchten)). One key theme is **authenticity in Church tradition**: the Ballerinis insist that genuine tradition must be distinguished from spurious accretions. They approach this in a systematic way, comparing the pseudo-decretals with genuine early letters and council canons. Their method is thoroughly scholarly: they identify phrases from the **Vulgate Bible and later sources** that appear in supposed 4th–5th-century papal letters, revealing the fraud. They also analyze historical context (e.g. references to church practices unknown in the alleged era) to pinpoint the collection’s true 9th-century origin. Theologically, the work touches on Church authority – ironically, by **rejecting these forged texts, the Ballerini strengthen the credibility of papal authority based on authentic sources** ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=moderne%20Forschung%20nimmt%20einen%20nordalpinen,PL%2056%2C%20246%E2%80%93248)).

**Relation to Patristics:** Although an 18th-century text, the dissertation is deeply connected to the Church Fathers and early councils. It was published in an edition of **Pope St. Leo I**, a prominent Father of the Church, and the Ballerinis had to discern Leo’s genuine writings from later interpolations. In their broader *Disquisitiones*, they survey canon-law collections from the **5th through 12th centuries**, placing the False Decretals in context ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=der%20ebenfalls%20bis%20heute%20verwendet,27)). This required familiarity with patristic documents – e.g. authentic letters of **popes and councils of the patristic era** against which Pseudo-Isidore could be compared. The Ballerinis’ earlier scholarly work included editing Church Fathers (they published the sermons of St. **Zeno of Verona** and collected works of Cardinal **Noris**, a patristic scholar) ([www.catholicity.com](https://www.catholicity.com/encyclopedia/b/ballerini%2Cgirolamo_and_pietro.html#:~:text=historical%20studies%20he%20soon%20came,Zeno%20of%20Verona)), which honed their critical skills. In the dissertation, they frequently cite genuine decretals of early popes like **Damasus, Siricius, Innocent I,** etc., as preserved in reputable collections, to show that the spurious texts were absent from early records. This aligns their work with the patristic revival of their time – they are upholding the true patristic heritage by clearing it of later forgery. While the dissertation itself does not engage in theological exegesis of Scripture or Fathers, it undergirds patristic studies by sorting authentic from inauthentic. In doing so, it affirms that the **Church Fathers and genuine early councils never saw these “Isidorian” letters**, bolstering the integrity of patristic tradition.

**Biblical Foundation:** The text is not primarily a Bible commentary, but scripture enters indirectly. The forgery purported to contain papal letters filled with scriptural quotations and church regulations. The Ballerinis point out cases where the **Latin Bible phrasing** in the False Decretals betrays a later version (e.g. the use of St. Jerome’s Vulgate in letters supposedly from the 300s). By highlighting such anachronistic biblical citations, they show the collection could not date from antiquity. The brothers’ commitment to truth was also rooted in a biblical ethic – implicitly, they uphold the command against bearing false witness. They occasionally invoke the ethos of early Church discipline (grounded in Scripture) as a contrast to the **legalistic tone of the forgeries**, which they argue reflects later medieval concerns. However, detailed scriptural exegesis is not a focus; rather, scripture is one more tool to test authenticity.

**Controversies and Debates:** The dissertation entered a contentious arena. The *False Decretals* had been a **source of controversy since the Reformation** – Protestant scholars from the 16th century onward (the Magdeburg Centuriators, then Blondel in 1628) had denounced them as forgeries, partly to undermine the historical arguments for papal supremacy. Until the 18th century, many Catholic scholars were defensive or silent on the issue. The Ballerinis broke with some contemporaries by openly acknowledging the falsification ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Hinsichtlich%20der%20Pseudoisidorischen%20F%C3%A4lschungen%20,Zugleich%20suchten)). This was daring: a few traditionalist Catholic writers still held the documents as genuine or at least preferred not to concede Protestant claims. By taking the Protestant allegations seriously and validating them with new evidence, the Ballerinis risked criticism from ultramontane hardliners. Indeed, their work can be seen as an intra-Catholic intervention in the **Gallican vs. ultramontane debate** – Gallican theologians (like Quesnel) had reason to expose the decretals as false, since those texts bolstered papal prerogatives; ultramontanes had been reluctant to abandon any prop of Papal authority. The Ballerini, however, managed to turn the debate: they *agreed* the decretals were fake, but argued this **did not damage the papacy’s legitimate authority**, since the Church never officially relied on forgeries ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=moderne%20Forschung%20nimmt%20einen%20nordalpinen,PL%2056%2C%20246%E2%80%93248)). They contended that the fraud was perpetrated for other reasons (protecting bishops), not by Rome itself ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=hinzu%2C%20dass%20es%20sich%20um,von%20Pseudoisidor%20gef%C3%A4lschte%20Dekretalen%20Teil)). In doing so, they neutralized a Protestant polemical weapon while defending Catholic ecclesiology on firmer ground. Their stance helped end the controversy over authenticity – after their work, few serious scholars (Catholic or Protestant) continued to claim Pseudo-Isidore was genuine. Another theological debate they engaged was the **Febronian** challenge to papal primacy in the 1760s. Pietro Ballerini’s later writings (e.g. *De vi ac ratione Primatus*, 1766) built on the dissertation’s outlook: he showed that **papal primacy rested on Scripture and true tradition, not on spurious decrees** ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=der%20Alpen%2C%20vielleicht%20in%20Mainz%3A,PL%2056%2C%20246%E2%80%93248)). This apologetic thread is present in *De collectione Isidori*, where they reassure readers that nothing in Catholic doctrine depends on these forged letters ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=,PL%2056%2C%20246%E2%80%93248)).

**Influence on Christian Thought:** The Ballerinis’ dissertation significantly advanced the **scientific study of church history and canon law**. It helped inaugurate a more critical attitude within the Catholic Church toward its own legal-historical sources. By demonstrating rigorous honesty, the brothers set a precedent: later Catholic scholars in the 19th century (like von Hefele, von Funk, or Archbishop Hefele) continued in the same vein of critical patristic scholarship, no longer fearing that truth would undermine the Church. In terms of specific impact, this work *shaped how the **Pseudo-Isidorian Decretals** have been understood ever since*. They influenced the first generation of professional church historians. For example, the great 19th-c. canonist **Friedrich Maassen** and others openly acknowledged that the Ballerinis had already solved the major questions about pre-Gratian canon collections ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=des%205,1)). Historian Johann von Schulte noted that for the early medieval period, the Ballerini research was so thorough that later scholars only had to glean details they left ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=des%205,1)). In short, the dissertation cemented the realization that **church authority had survived despite forgeries, not because of them**. This contributed to a more robust Catholic position in modern debates: the papacy could renounce falsified “evidence” and rest on authentic tradition. The work also indirectly fed into the reform of canon law – by the 19th and 20th centuries, when the Church codified canon law, the Pseudo-Isidorean material (though it had influenced medieval law via Gratian) was long recognized as invalid. The Ballerinis’ careful scholarship was a stepping stone toward that purification. In a broader intellectual sense, *De collectione Isidori Mercatoris* exemplified the Catholic Enlightenment approach: it embraced reason and critical methods to deepen faith’s historical understanding, showing that **faith and critical scholarship need not be at odds**. Later generations of ecclesiastical historians consistently cite the Ballerinis in studies of papal letters, councils, and the False Decretals, affirming the lasting value of their contribution.

## Manuscript Tradition and Editions

**Manuscript Witnesses:** Since *De collectione Isidori Mercatoris* is an 18th-century scholarly essay, it does not have a manuscript tradition in the way ancient texts do. The work was **composed and published in print** by the Ballerini brothers, not transmitted in medieval manuscripts. However, the strength of the dissertation lies in the Ballerinis’ extensive use of primary manuscript sources. To build their case, they collated numerous **medieval manuscripts of canon law collections** – including codices of genuine decretals and earlier collections that Pasquier Quesnel had not consulted ([www.catholicity.com](https://www.catholicity.com/encyclopedia/b/ballerini%2Cgirolamo_and_pietro.html#:~:text=After%20almost%20nine%20years%20of,the%20canons%2C%20together%20with%20some)). In their preface, they note that Quesnel’s edition overlooked many important manuscripts, which they then examined ([www.catholicity.com](https://www.catholicity.com/encyclopedia/b/ballerini%2Cgirolamo_and_pietro.html#:~:text=After%20almost%20nine%20years%20of,the%20canons%2C%20together%20with%20some)). For example, they drew on codices of the **Collectio Quesnelliana, Dionysiana, Hispana,** etc., and identified the earliest manuscripts of Pseudo-Isidore (all dating from the mid-9th century or later). They observed that no manuscript evidence of the Isidorian decretals appeared before about 850 AD, a key clue to its spurious nature. Thus, while the dissertation itself isn’t a copied text, it synthesizes evidence from a wide array of medieval manuscripts. Modern scholars often credit the Ballerinis for effectively doing a “manuscript tradition study” of the False Decretals – they traced how those texts suddenly appear in the historical record and in which libraries (concluding an origin in the Carolingian Empire, likely in the Rhineland or Gaul ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=hinzu%2C%20dass%20es%20sich%20um,von%20Pseudoisidor%20gef%C3%A4lschte%20Dekretalen%20Teil))).

**Printed Editions:** The original publication of the dissertation was in **1757 in Verona** (often listed as Venice 1757, since the printing was under the Venetian domain). It appeared as **Part III, Chapter VI of the Ballerinis’ *Tractatus de antiquis canonum collectionibus*** – a treatise appended to volume 3 of *Sancti Leonis Magni Opera Omnia*. This first edition can be found in the 3rd tome’s pages i–cccxx (320 pages of Latin text and notes) ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=,nur%20%E2%80%9EBallerini%E2%80%9C%20zitierte%20Analyse%20der)). In the 19th century, **J.-P. Migne** reprinted the entire Ballerini *Disquisitiones* in his *Patrologia Latina* series. The dissertation *De collectione Isidori Mercatoris* is included in **Patrologia Latina volume 56**, spanning columns 11–354 in Migne’s double-column format ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=quatuor%20partes%20distributus,ratione%20primatus%20romanorum%20pontificum%2C%20Verona)). The Migne text (published 1846) is essentially a photographic reprint of the Ballerinis’ edition and is considered reliable, though it lacks the critical apparatus of the original (such as variant readings or extensive footnotes). Scholars often cite the work via *PL 56*. No newer critical edition has been made, largely because the Ballerinis’ work is already of high quality **and the topic (Pseudo-Isidore) has been covered in modern research articles rather than standalone editions**. The Latin text can be accessed today through digitizations (for example, Google Books and Archive.org have scans of *PL 56* and the original 1757 edition) ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=,40%20digitale)) ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=,nur%20%E2%80%9EBallerini%E2%80%9C%20zitierte%20Analyse%20der)).

**Translations:** As an academic Latin dissertation embedded in a larger work, *De collectione Isidori Mercatoris* has not, to our knowledge, been fully translated into English or other modern languages. Its readership has been limited to specialists who can handle the Latin. Key findings and excerpts have been summarized in modern languages within secondary literature. For instance, 19th-century German and French historians of canon law paraphrased the Ballerinis’ arguments in their own works (e.g. von Schulte in German, or in the French *Dictionnaire de Droit Canonique* entry on the Ballerinis ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=des%205,1))). But a standalone translation does not exist – researchers refer directly to the Latin text in *PL* or the 1757 edition. Today, scholars of Pseudo-Isidore often rely on the Ballerinis’ Latin or on summaries in works like *History of Canon Law* by F. Maassen (which praised and built on the Ballerinis ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=des%205,1))).

**Textual Issues:** Because the dissertation was a printed work by its authors, there are no variant manuscript readings of the text itself. The Ballerinis oversaw its production, so the 1757 print is authoritative. Migne’s reprint from 1846 is virtually identical, save for minor formatting differences (Migne numbered the columns and sometimes abbreviated citations). One consideration is that the original edition contained extensive learned footnotes citing sources and manuscripts, which Migne either reproduces in smaller font or sometimes incorporates into the text. Modern readers should be aware that **Migne’s version lacks the context of the original volumes** – for example, the Ballerinis’ preface and the broader *Disquisitiones* structure. When consulting the text, using the original edition’s page numbers or the *PL* column numbers is necessary for citation (the standard citation is *PL 56, cols. 241–251* for the core of the Isidore dissertation, for instance ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Hinsichtlich%20der%20Pseudoisidorischen%20F%C3%A4lschungen%20,der%20F%C3%A4lschungen%20an%20und%20betont))). In terms of content, the Latin style is clear and scholastic, but dense with references. No significant errors are known in the printed text; later scholars have confirmed that the Ballerinis’ quotations from medieval sources were impressively accurate for their time. In summary, the text survives in excellent form through its 18th-century printing, and all subsequent references to *De collectione Isidori Mercatoris* ultimately go back to the Ballerinis’ published version, as reprinted in *Patrologia Latina* vol. 56.

## Author Biography

**Life and Education:** **Pietro Ballerini** (born 7 September 1698, died 28 March 1769) and his younger brother **Girolamo Ballerini** (born 29 January 1701, died 23 April 1781) were natives of **Verona**, in the Republic of Venice ([www.catholic.org](https://www.catholic.org/encyclopedia/view.php?id=1435#:~:text=,to%20assist%20each%20other%20in)). They were sons of a distinguished Veronese surgeon, and grew up in an intellectually rich environment ([www.newadvent.org](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02223b.htm#:~:text=Celebrated%20theologians%20%20and%20canonists%2C,9%2C%20also%20of%20Verona%2C%20and)). Both brothers received a rigorous classical and theological education at the Jesuit college in Verona ([www.newadvent.org](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02223b.htm#:~:text=Celebrated%20theologians%20%20and%20canonists%2C,9%2C%20also%20of%20Verona%2C%20and)). After schooling, **Girolamo** entered the diocesan seminary and was ordained a priest (in 1725) ([www.encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ballerini-pietro-and-girolamo#:~:text=Patristic%20scholars%20and%20theologians%3B%20Pietro%2C,scholarship%20consisted%20in%20a%20close)), while **Pietro** was ordained in 1722 and initially became headmaster of a classics school in Verona ([www.encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ballerini-pietro-and-girolamo#:~:text=Patristic%20scholars%20and%20theologians%3B%20Pietro%2C,scholarship%20consisted%20in%20a%20close)). The two had a very close partnership from an early age – contemporaries remarked on their “rare intellectual sympathy” and constant collaboration ([www.newadvent.org](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02223b.htm#:~:text=Celebrated%20theologians%20%20and%20canonists%2C,9%2C%20also%20of%20Verona%2C%20and)). They never joined a religious order; they remained secular (diocesan) priests and scholars. This position, on the fringes of ecclesiastical bureaucracy, gave them freedom to pursue massive research projects together.

**Career and Works:** The Ballerini brothers emerged as leading Catholic scholars of the 18th century. Early on, **Girolamo** focused on editing historical works: between 1729 and 1733 he published a five-volume complete edition of the works of Cardinal **Enrico Noris** ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Girolamo%20ver%C3%B6ffentlichte%201729%E2%80%9341%20die%20gesammelten,Seine%20Polemik)) ([www.catholicity.com](https://www.catholicity.com/encyclopedia/b/ballerini%2Cgirolamo_and_pietro.html#:~:text=historical%20studies%20he%20soon%20came,Zeno%20of%20Verona)) – Noris was a famed 17th-century scholar (and incidentally an anti-Jansenist polemicist) who became a model for the brothers’ scholarly rigor. Girolamo also brought out an edition of the writings of **Matteo Giberti**, a reforming Bishop of Verona, in 1733, and a critical edition of **St. Zeno of Verona’s sermons** in 1739 ([www.catholicity.com](https://www.catholicity.com/encyclopedia/b/ballerini%2Cgirolamo_and_pietro.html#:~:text=edition%20of%20his%20works,Zeno%20of%20Verona)). **Pietro**, meanwhile, made his mark in theology and canon law. In 1724 he wrote a treatise on the educational method of St. Augustine (for use in his school) – in doing so he entered a then-heated debate over moral theology. He advocated “probabiliorism” (the idea that in moral uncertainty one should follow the more probable/right opinion) against the extreme **Probabilists**, sparking a published controversy over the next decade ([www.catholicity.com](https://www.catholicity.com/encyclopedia/b/ballerini%2Cgirolamo_and_pietro.html#:~:text=Verona,1774%29%20one%20of%20the)). In 1747 Pietro published *De iure divino et naturali circa usuram* (“On Divine and Natural Law regarding Usury”) ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Augustinus%20und%20gab%20unter%20anderem,sich%20hier%20speziell%20gegen%20%E2%80%9EFebronius%E2%80%9C)), a six-book work condemning all forms of usury. Interestingly, a few propositions in this anti-usury work were later found objectionable by Pope Benedict XIV and were condemned in the papal bull *Vix pervenit* (1745) ([www.encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ballerini-pietro-and-girolamo#:~:text=were%20born%20and%20educated%20in,Cardinal%20Henry%20Noris%2C%20a%20Veronese)) – possibly because Pietro’s strict stance inadvertently clashed with the nuanced official position on lending. Nevertheless, Pietro’s scholarly reputation only grew. He prepared critical editions of two major theological works: the *Summa Theologica* of St. **Antoninus of Florence** (published 1740–41) ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Girolamo%20ver%C3%B6ffentlichte%201729%E2%80%9341%20die%20gesammelten,Seine%20Polemik)) and the *Summa de poenitentia* of **Raymund of Peñafort** (published 1744) ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Girolamo%20ver%C3%B6ffentlichte%201729%E2%80%9341%20die%20gesammelten,Seine%20Polemik)) – both aimed at providing reliable texts for moral theology and canon law. By the mid-1740s, the brothers were widely respected in Italian intellectual circles.

A turning point came in **1748**, when the Republic of Venice asked Pietro Ballerini to serve as its **representative (canonist) in Rome** during a jurisdictional dispute with the Patriarchate of Aquileia ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=studierten%20am%20%C3%B6rtlichen%20Jesuitenkolleg%20,den%20Papst%20in%20kirchenrechtlichen%20und)). Pietro’s performance in Rome impressed Pope **Benedict XIV**. That same year, Benedict XIV – a pope known for patronizing scholarship – commissioned Pietro to undertake a new, *definitive edition of **Pope Leo I**’s letters and works*, explicitly to **replace Pasquier Quesnel’s flawed edition** ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Republik%20Venedig%20%20damit%20beauftragt%2C,den%20Papst%20in%20kirchenrechtlichen%20und)) ([www.encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ballerini-pietro-and-girolamo#:~:text=1740%E2%80%9341,a%20history%20of%20probabilism%2C%20Saggio)). Pietro and Girolamo devoted the next several years to this enormous project. Pietro relocated to Rome for research, while Girolamo likely assisted from Verona. In **1753–57**, they published **three volumes of *Leo the Great’s Opera Omnia*** (printed in Venice) with extensive critical apparatus ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Gemeinsam%20publizierten%20die%20beiden%20Br%C3%BCder,hoher%20Qualit%C3%A4t%20gewesen%20sei%2C%20dass)). This edition was a triumph: it not only included Leo’s authentic writings (corrected by consulting many manuscripts) but also *added a third volume of dissertations* – among them the *De collectione Isidori Mercatoris*. The brothers’ methodology set a new standard, and their Leo edition **“has not been superseded to this day”** ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Gemeinsam%20publizierten%20die%20beiden%20Br%C3%BCder,hoher%20Qualit%C3%A4t%20gewesen%20sei%2C%20dass)). (Indeed, no later edition of Leo I would appear until the late 20th century, so scholars long relied on “the Ballerini edition.”) The third volume also reprinted and critiqued Quesnel’s collection of canons (the *Collectio Quesnelliana*) ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=durch%20eine%20bessere%20ersetzt%20worden,hoher%20Qualit%C3%A4t%20gewesen%20sei%2C%20dass)), and surveyed numerous early canonical collections in a comprehensive study (*Disquisitiones de collectionibus*). The impact was immediate: learned journals across Europe recognized the Ballerinis’ edition as a cornerstone of patristic and legal scholarship.

In their later years, the Ballerinis continued writing on contemporary church controversies. Pietro, especially, turned his attention to defending the papacy against the rising Gallican and Febronian challenges. In **1765** he published *De potestate ecclesiastica summorum Pontificum et conciliorum generalium* (“On the Ecclesiastical Power of the Supreme Pontiffs and of General Councils”), and in **1766** *De vi ac ratione Primatus Romanorum Pontificum* (“On the Force and Nature of the Primacy of the Roman Pontiffs”) ([www.encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ballerini-pietro-and-girolamo#:~:text=Patrologia%20Latina%20v,1765)) ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=usurani%20libri%20sex%20,sich%20hier%20speziell%20gegen%20%E2%80%9EFebronius%E2%80%9C)). These works directly rebutted the ideas of “**Febronius**” (the pseudonym of Bishop Johann N. von Hontheim), who had argued for limiting papal authority. Drawing on church history (and no doubt informed by their studies of genuine vs. fake documents), Pietro made a strong case for the traditional papal primacy, detached from suspect pseudo-historical claims. Girolamo, for his part, may have assisted with these works, though his name is not on them. In 1769 Pietro died in Verona; Girolamo lived on until 1781, continuing to teach and do smaller editing projects. It’s worth noting that the brothers remained in **Verona** throughout their careers (aside from Pietro’s sojourns to Rome). They never sought high ecclesiastical office; instead, they were *scholar-priests*. Girolamo ran a school in Verona for many years and was known as a humble and holy priest in addition to his scholarship ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Der%20Vater%20von%20Pietro%20und,den%20Papst%20in%20kirchenrechtlichen%20und)).

**Historical Context and Contemporaries:** The Ballerinis lived under the Venetian Republic’s rule, which, while Catholic, often tussled with the papacy on jurisdiction. This may have influenced the brothers to firmly articulate papal rights (their writings against Febronianism and Gallicanism show a strong ultramontane bent ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Die%20Traktate%2C%20Quellenpublikationen%20und%20teils,der%20%2031%20Collectio%20Dionysio))). They were part of a circle of learned northern Italian clergy that included figures like **Scipione Maffei** and **Jacopo Vallarsi** (who edited works of Jerome) – a group devoted to patristic and historical studies in the 18th century. The Ballerinis were also in correspondence or dialogue with scholars in other countries: they were aware of Protestant scholarship and built upon it where it did not conflict with faith. This was somewhat daring at the time – cooperating indirectly with Protestant researchers’ findings – but it exemplified the **Catholic Enlightenment** approach to truth. Politically, they lived through the repression of Jansenism (they applauded the papal condemnations of Jansenists like Quesnel) and through the emergence of state-church conflicts. Their defense of Roman primacy in the 1760s came as European rulers like the Habsburgs were curtailing some papal influence; the Ballerinis provided theological ammunition in favor of the papacy during these debates. Notably, the brothers always remained on the right side of Church authorities (the minor issue of Pietro’s usury theses aside). Popes Benedict XIV and later Clement XIII held them in esteem. After Pietro’s death, Girolamo saw the rise of new challenges (the suppression of the Jesuits in 1773, etc.), but by then his active production had ceased. 

**Legacy:** The Ballerini brothers are remembered as **giants of ecclesiastical scholarship**. Their meticulous research and critical editions earned them a place in the scholarly lineage alongside the likes of the French Maurists and other great editors of patristic texts. In the field of **canon law history**, their *Disquisitiones* is still cited – in fact, 19th-century historians like von Schulte remarked that the Ballerinis did foundational work on early canon collections that scarcely needed improvement ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=des%205,1)). The term “the Ballerini” in canon-law circles often refers specifically to their analysis of pre-Gratian collections (including Pseudo-Isidore), which had a towering reputation ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=des%205,1)). Their edition of Leo the Great remained the standard until modern critical editions: it was reprinted in Migne and widely used by scholars and clergy for over a century. The brothers also had an impact on theological thought: by clearing away forged materials, they helped the Church base its teachings on more solid historical ground. Their anti-Jansenist, anti-Gallican writings contributed to the eventual triumph of the ultramontane view (culminating in the definition of papal primacy and infallibility in 1870 – concepts they defended long before it was fashionable). Locally in Verona, the Ballerini are counted among the illustrious sons of the city; their portraits and biographies appear in 18th/19th-century collections of notable Veronese. Scholars Ovidio Capitani and others in the 20th century have entries on them in encyclopedias, testifying to their enduring scholarly significance ([dewiki.de](https://dewiki.de/Lexikon/Pietro_und_Girolamo_Ballerini#:~:text=Literatur)). In summary, **Pietro and Girolamo Ballerini** exemplify the erudition and faithfulness of early modern Catholic scholarship. They bridged the gap between rigorous historical inquiry and orthodox theology, leaving a legacy of honesty and excellence that has benefited the study of church history ever since.
