  <p>This anonymous sermon for the feast of a confessor survives in Yale University’s Beinecke MS 571, where it occupies ff. 65r–69v. The sermon was copied in the second half of the tenth century in a large Carolingian minuscule. The original scribe leaves the saint unnamed, using the placeholder ill. wherever a name would normally appear.</p>
  <p>A later, twelfth-century reader supplied Aderaldi above the placeholder in two places, apparently associating the sermon with Aderald of Troyes. That identification is not secure. The sermon itself refers to the confessor as a bishop, while Aderald was an archdeacon. The annotation is therefore preserved here as part of the manuscript’s later history rather than incorporated into the original text.</p>

The Latin published here was transcribed directly from the manuscript images, independently reread, and reconciled against the images. Later corrections and additions are represented in the reading text where they form the manuscript’s final transmitted wording, with the earlier readings recorded separately in the apparatus.</p>

## Historical Context

### Authorship

The responsible attribution is **Anonymous**. This is not a pseudonymous work currently assigned to Augustine, Maximus, Caesarius, or another Father: the rubric merely reads *Sermo in natale unius confessoris*, and the Yale catalogue calls it an “unidentified sermon.” No author’s name occurs in the portion described at ff. 65r–69v. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="pre1600ms.beinecke.library.yale.edu">[1]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="pre1600ms.beinecke.library.yale.edu">[1]</a>

There is not yet a scholarly consensus about authorship in the stronger sense, because no dedicated edition or source study has been located. “Anonymous” therefore describes the present state of the evidence rather than a demonstrated authorial identity. Comparisons should eventually be made against Caesarius of Arles, the Eusebius Gallicanus collection, Pseudo-Augustine, Pseudo-Maximus, and early Carolingian sermonaries, but no verbal parallel currently justifies reassignment.

### Date

The secure date is that of the **copy**, not necessarily the sermon’s original composition. Yale dates hand B—the scribe responsible for ff. 64r–74v—to the **second half of the tenth century**; Bernhard Bischoff proposed the early eleventh century, which Albert Derolez regarded as too late because of the script’s archaic appearance. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="pre1600ms.beinecke.library.yale.edu">[1]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="pre1600ms.beinecke.library.yale.edu">[1]</a>

Accordingly, the safest formulation is: **the surviving copy was written in the later tenth century, while the sermon may be earlier**. Its language about rescue *de gladiis paganorum* and an “unhoped-for peace” could fit the ninth- or tenth-century aftermath of Scandinavian, Magyar, or Saracen violence, but this is an inference, not a dateable historical reference. Frankish annals commonly called Scandinavian raiders *pagani*, yet the term itself is not geographically diagnostic. <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="conquest, however, their very existence also had a profound psychological impact upon the collective Christian ethos of European society.⁴⁰ The ninth century was an age when many writers in the Christian West were morbidly preoccupied with the castigation of sins and the pagani were understood to be the agents of divine retribution for religious and moral decay.⁴¹ The entire fabric of Western Christian society was perceived by some to be in danger of unravelling before these instruments of God’s displeasure, as various contemporary texts illustrate. One notable author of such religiously-motivated writing was King Alfred himself. Alfred, was not, however, the first West Saxon king to find himself at the mercy of the Northmen; nor was he the first to engage them in warfare and to inflict a considerable defeat upon them. Alfred was not even the first West Saxon king who moralised about the possibility of divine retribution for the neglect of spiritual observance.⁴²">[2]</a>

### Provenance

The manuscript is catalogued as French, but the sermon’s place of composition and copying remains undetermined. Nordenfalk’s Fleury attribution concerns the decoration of the earlier Gregory section, not necessarily hand B or the sermon. Likewise, Troyes is suggested only by the later Aderald gloss and the reported Pierre Pithou provenance. The catalogue’s statement that the Aderald annotation “may point to Poitiers” should be reported but cannot presently be reconciled with its identification of Aderald as the archdeacon of Troyes. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="pre1600ms.beinecke.library.yale.edu">[1]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="pre1600ms.beinecke.library.yale.edu">[1]</a>

### Historical setting and audience

The sermon belongs to the adaptable literature of the **Common of Saints**, particularly the Common of a Confessor or Confessor-Bishop. Carolingian homiliaries included headings such as *in depositione unius confessoris*, *de confessoribus*, and even a sermon for “Saint Martin or another confessor.” <a class="cite" href="#ref-3" title="Handschriften / Homiliarium Pauli Diaconi - Cod. Aug. perg. 19">[3]</a> Such rubrics allowed a text to be reused for saints lacking fully proper liturgical material.

Its rhetorical audience is a mixed crowd assembled at a shrine: the morally serious, the sick, pilgrims seeking cures, and visitors bringing candles or offerings without inward conversion. Yet the manuscript’s lesson divisions and *Tu autem* cue show that this preaching text was subsequently reused as Office reading. The original sermon and its later division for the Night Office should therefore be distinguished.

## Theological Significance

The sermon’s organizing contrast is between **external pilgrimage and interior conversion**. Some visitors seek bodily healing; some carry lights and offerings; others come in genuine repentance. The preacher does not reject pilgrimage, relics, miracles, or saintly intercession. Instead, he insists that these practices must be governed by faith, hope, charity, penance, and obedience.

A second major theme is the **communion and intercession of the saints**. The confessor stands within the heavenly society of angels, apostles, martyrs, confessors, and virgins, interceding for the living. The saint is not an independent source of power: the conclusion praises God, “who is always wonderful in his saints.” That hierarchy—God as giver, saints as intercessors—keeps the cult doctrinally conventional. The sermon’s final designation of the saint as *confessor atque pontifex* indicates that its adaptable subject was a bishop-confessor. This is precisely where the Aderald identification fails historically: Aderald was a canon and archdeacon, not a bishop.

The sermon also contains a compact **history of grace**: creation, redemption, baptism, the episcopal laying on of hands with the sevenfold Spirit, and restoration through penance. This sequence is broadly Augustinian in making divine gifts prior to the believer’s moral response, although no direct borrowing from Augustine has been demonstrated. Its confirmation theology is mainstream Latin: the baptized are presented to bishops, who confer or invoke the Holy Spirit by prayer and imposition of hands, traditionally described through Isaiah’s sevenfold gifts. <a class="cite" href="#ref-6" title="CATHOLIC ENCYCLOPEDIA: Confirmation">[6]</a> This material would have been useful catechesis for a mixed lay congregation.

The biblical structure is predominantly moral and pastoral. Romans 12:1–2 supports transformation rather than conformity to the world; Matthew 6:33 orders material needs under the search for God’s kingdom; Matthew 16:26 asks what profit there is in gaining the world while losing the soul; and Galatians 5 supplies the catalogue of the works of the flesh. The sick who are blind, deaf, lame, paralyzed, possessed, or mentally afflicted also evoke the Gospel healing catalogues. The preacher repeatedly converts physical conditions into moral diagnosis: one may bring a visible lamp while remaining inwardly dark or seek bodily liberation while still serving the devil.

No heterodox theology is evident. The strongest potential interpretive issue is not doctrine but historical allusion. “Rescue from the swords of the pagans” may reflect remembered Northman attacks, but Magyars or Saracens cannot be excluded without provenance. Nor has an exact parallel for *huius insperate pacis* been located. Finally, there is no evidence that the sermon influenced Christian thought on a wider scale. Its demonstrable reception is local and material: a later reader associated it with Aderald, revisers altered it, and liturgical users divided it into lessons.

## Manuscript Tradition and Editions

The exact text is presently located in **New Haven, Beinecke Library, MS 571, ff. 65r–69v**. Yale describes it as article 3 of a composite manuscript containing Gregory the Great’s *Dialogi*, the end of Sulpicius Severus’s third letter on Martin, this sermon, and a *Vita S. Symeonis Stylitae*. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="pre1600ms.beinecke.library.yale.edu">[1]</a>. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="pre1600ms.beinecke.library.yale.edu">[1]</a>

The Beinecke manuscript is the **only witness so far located for this exact incipit**, but this is a search-state report, not a uniqueness claim. Manuscripts indexed under the same generic title may contain unrelated texts. Particularly important outstanding searches are *In Principio*, Mirabile, Grégoire’s repertories, Machielsen’s *Clavis Patristica Pseudepigraphorum Medii Aevi*, sermon databases, and full incipit/explicit searches in uncatalogued homiliaries.

This is **not currently a Patrologia Latina text**: no PL volume can responsibly be assigned. As of September 21, 2026, no edition or translation has been located in PL, CSEL, CCSL, *Sources Chrétiennes*, or the bounded databases described in the prompt. It should not be described as “never edited” without exhaustive repertory work.

The principal textual problems are the repeated *ill.* placeholders, later supralinear “Aderaldi” additions, correction by a revising hand, two struck passages, abbreviated or uncertain lesson numbering, and the physical deterioration of the parchment. An edition should distinguish at least three strata: hand B’s base text, the reviser’s interventions, and the later Aderald/liturgical annotations. High-resolution multispectral or raking-light inspection may clarify cancellations and faint lesson marks.

## Author Biography

No conventional biography can be written for the author. There is no recoverable name, birthplace, education, ecclesiastical office, career, or date of death. Assigning the sermon to one of the major patristic sermon collections would therefore be premature.

A limited intellectual profile can nevertheless be inferred from the text. The author or compiler was a Latin cleric with practical command of Scripture, elementary sacramental theology, moral exhortation, saintly intercession, penitential discipline, and ecclesiastical finance. The treatment of baptism, episcopal hand-laying, the sevenfold Spirit, and penance suggests access to clerical catechetical traditions rather than merely hagiographic storytelling. The injunction concerning tithes and first-fruits points in the same pastoral direction. None of these features requires the author to have been a bishop; a priest, canon, monk, cathedral cleric, or compiler of model sermons could have drawn upon them.

The direct address—*Fratres karissimi*—and detailed classification of pilgrims create the impression of a sermon delivered at a crowded shrine. Nevertheless, written sermons often preserve fictional or conventional preaching situations. It is safer to speak of the sermon’s **rhetorical congregation** than of a documented performance. Its adaptable placeholders also suggest that it could have functioned as a model text designed for reuse at different churches.

The anonymous writer should be placed within the broad pastoral inheritance of late antique and Carolingian Gaul. Caesarius of Arles popularized concise moral preaching directed toward ordinary Christians; the Eusebius Gallicanus sermons transmitted adaptable Gallic exhortation; and Carolingian compilers reorganized patristic material according to the liturgical year. Paul the Deacon’s homiliary, commissioned in Charlemagne’s orbit, included a *Commune sanctorum*. <a class="cite" href="#ref-8" title="Brepols - The Homiliary of Paul the Deacon">[8]</a> These are contexts, not proven sources.

The author’s most defensible “legacy” lies in the sermon’s continued adaptability. A later community could divide it for nocturnal reading, revise its wording, and apply it to Aderald despite the mismatch between Aderald’s archidiaconate and the text’s episcopal saint. The manuscript thus preserves not merely an anonymous composition but a history of changing liturgical use.
