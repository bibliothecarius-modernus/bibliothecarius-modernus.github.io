## Historical Context

The attribution of the *De expositione missae*—also transmitted as the *Opusculum de actione missarum*—to **Florus of Lyon is secure by normal medieval standards and is not currently treated as pseudonymous**. Vatican, Reg. lat. 214 carries the old attribution *Opusculum de actione missarum collectum a Floro magistro*, while other witnesses identify Florus as *magister* or Lyonnais deacon. The work’s prologue also describes exactly the patristic compilation method associated with Florus elsewhere: extracts are assembled from named Fathers, with marginal letters identifying their authors. Duc, 1937, p. 41, “*collectum a Floro magistro*”: https://archive.org/details/Duc1937Florus. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="Full text of &quot;Paul Duc : L'Expositio Missae de Florus de Lyon…&quot;">[1]</a>

Its date is less certain. Paul Duc argued for **833–834**, interpreting it as a response intended to counter the influence of Amalarius of Metz before Agobard’s exile in 835. Jean-Paul Bouhot’s source-critical chronology placed it somewhat earlier: shortly after Amalarius added his third, Mass-related book to the *Liber officialis* and presented it to Louis the Pious in 823—about 825. A cautious working date is therefore **c. 825 to 835**, rather than 835–850. The explicitly polemical writings against Amalarius belong to 835–839 and should not automatically determine the date of the less overtly polemical *Expositio*. Duc, pp. 55–56: https://archive.org/details/Duc1937Florus; Bouhot, pp. 168–169: https://www.mgh-bibliothek.de/dokumente/b/b042280.pdf. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="Full text of &quot;Paul Duc : L'Expositio Missae de Florus de Lyon…&quot;">[1]</a>

The probable place of composition is **the cathedral church of Lyon**, where Florus worked as deacon, teacher, textual scholar, and adviser within a particularly active clerical and manuscript culture. This provenance is an inference from Florus’s career, the Lyonnais character of his intellectual work, and his engagement with local liturgical policy, rather than a surviving authorial colophon. Modern study of Florus’s manuscripts emphasizes the unusually rich evidence for his use and production of books in the cathedral library. Chambert-Protat thesis abstract, EPHE *Répertoire des thèses soutenues en 2018*, p. 20: https://www.ephe.psl.eu/sites/default/files/2021-10/R%C3%A9pertoire%20des%20th%C3%A8ses%20206%20%C3%A0%202018.pdf. <a class="cite" href="#ref-2" title="Pierre CHAMBERT-PROTAT">[2]</a>

The immediate setting was the Carolingian effort to regularize clerical education and worship, complicated at Lyon by political conflict. Agobard opposed Louis the Pious during the imperial civil wars and was removed in 835; Amalarius then administered Lyon and attempted liturgical changes. Florus, loyal to Agobard and suspicious of Amalarius’s speculative symbolism, became a principal opponent. His campaign culminated in the scrutiny and condemnation of Amalarius’s disputed teachings at Quierzy in 838. The sequence is documented here: https://geschichtsquellen.de/werk/2281?s=en#:~:text=Zwischen%20835%20und%20839,Anklage%20des%20Florus. <a class="cite" href="#ref-3" title="Geschichtsquellen: Werk/2281">[3]</a>

The likely primary audience was **educated clergy, especially priests expected to understand and explain the prayers they recited**. Florus does not write a ceremonial handbook: he normally passes over bodily gestures, vestments, and processions in order to interpret the theological *ratio* of the Mass. His aim was to equip clergy—and through them congregations—with a patristically grounded understanding of worship, resisting imaginative explanations that Florus regarded as doctrinally unstable. Duc, pp. 57–58: https://archive.org/details/Duc1937Florus. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="Full text of &quot;Paul Duc : L'Expositio Missae de Florus de Lyon…&quot;">[1]</a>

## Theological Significance

The *Expositio* comments on the complete sequence covered by the present two-part series: §§1–42 move from the prologue through *Dominus vobiscum*, *Sursum corda*, the preface, and *Sanctus*; §§43–92 interpret the Roman Canon from *Te igitur*, then the Lord’s Prayer, *Libera nos*, peace, *Agnus Dei*, communion, and dismissal. Florus treats these texts less as a sequence of dramatic symbols than as a doctrinally coherent act of the Church.

Its defining method is **patristic florilegium**. The prologue names Cyprian, Ambrose, Augustine, Jerome, Gregory, Fulgentius, Severianus, Vigilius, Isidore, Bede, and Avitus, together with “ancient books of the mysteries.” Marginal author letters enabled the reader to recognize the source without repeatedly interrupting the prose. Florus says that his concern is not primarily the wording—which is comparatively plain—but the *ratio et actio* of the mystery: the faith with which it must be celebrated and the devotion with which it must be received. Primary text: https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/De_expositione_missae#:~:text=Incipit%20opusculum%20de%20actione,Aviti. <a class="cite" href="#ref-4" title="De expositione missae - Wikisource">[4]</a>

**Augustine is the dominant theological voice.** Florus organizes Augustinian themes—sacrifice, sign and reality, ecclesial unity, charity, remembrance, and participation—around the actual prayers of the Mass. His work is therefore not a neutral chain of excerpts. Duc observed that Florus selected, connected, and reshaped inherited material into a doctrinal synthesis. Recent scholarship on Florus’s “Augustinian centos” likewise emphasizes his precision and editorial agency rather than treating him as a mechanical copyist. Duc, pp. 59 and 72: https://archive.org/details/Duc1937Florus; Chambert-Protat, “Les centons augustiniens”: https://doi.org/10.1484/J.REA.5.107875. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="Full text of &quot;Paul Duc : L'Expositio Missae de Florus de Lyon…&quot;">[1]</a>

The main theological themes are the Eucharist as the Church’s true sacrifice of praise and thanksgiving; Christ as both priest and victim; the sacramental commemoration of the Passion; the unity of Christ’s ecclesial body; intercession for living and dead; and communion as the completion of the sacrificial action. Biblical foundations include the institution narratives and 1 Corinthians 11:23–26, Paul’s teaching that the many become one bread and one body in 1 Corinthians 10:16–17, the Lord’s Prayer in Matthew 6, Old Testament sacrifices interpreted typologically, and psalmic language of praise, ascent, purity, and thanksgiving.

The work’s most important controversial context is Florus’s opposition to **Amalarius’s allegorical method and disputed account of the “threefold body” of Christ**. The *Expositio* itself is restrained compared with Florus’s later denunciations. It is best described as a constructive patristic alternative: where Amalarius often associated liturgical persons, objects, and gestures with episodes of salvation history, Florus concentrated on the received prayers and their theological meaning. Duc’s claim that the work was deliberately written to neutralize the *Liber officialis* is influential but remains a scholarly interpretation, not an authorial declaration. The explicit anti-Amalarian campaign belongs to 835–839. <a class="cite" href="#ref-3" title="Geschichtsquellen: Werk/2281">[3]</a>

Its influence is visible both in manuscript diffusion and in later theological reception. Duc identified parallels with Hincmar of Reims and argued that Hincmar adopted Florus’s formulations concerning Christ as priest and victim and the words of consecration. The broader Carolingian tradition continued through works associated with Auxerre, including Remigius, and through later medieval commentaries on the Canon; nevertheless, particular lines of dependence should be demonstrated passage by passage rather than inferred from shared patristic sources. Duc, pp. 78–83: https://archive.org/details/Duc1937Florus. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="Full text of &quot;Paul Duc : L'Expositio Missae de Florus de Lyon…&quot;">[1]</a>

## Manuscript Tradition and Editions

**Base text.** The translation is made from Migne, *Patrologia Latina* 119, cols. 15A–72C, the practical public-domain base. PL reprints the older printed text of E. Martène and U. Durand, *Veterum scriptorum et monumentorum … amplissima collectio*, vol. IX (Paris 1733), which Martène presented as printed from a manuscript of Queen Christina’s collection; Duc showed that from about §53 Martène also drew on other sources, chiefly the Lyon *Maxima Bibliotheca Patrum*, and that the long developments in §§77–83 are not in that manuscript. P. Duc identified that manuscript as Vatican, Reg. lat. 194 and dated its Florus text to the late eleventh century (Martène had said “tenth century at least”). Before Martène, the *Bibliotheca Patrum* printed a much shorter text (Martène: “not a quarter” of the work). <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="Full text of &quot;Paul Duc : L'Expositio Missae de Florus de Lyon…&quot;">[1]</a>

2. **Known defects.** Paul Duc, *Étude sur l’Expositio missae de Florus de Lyon, suivie d’une édition critique du texte* (Belley 1937), edited the work from Troyes 804 (s. IX ex.), controlled by Paris, BnF lat. 12279 (s. IX) and Vatican, Reg. lat. 214 (s. X). On pp. 47–50 he listed the places where the Martène/PL text omits, garbles, displaces or interpolates: the omissions in §§53, 55, 57, 58, 64–65, 68, 70, 72–74, 89, 90, 92; the muddled §60 (institution narrative) and §76; the interpolated developments on the Lord’s Prayer petitions in §§77–83; and a list of wrong readings. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="Full text of &quot;Paul Duc : L'Expositio Missae de Florus de Lyon…&quot;">[1]</a>

3. **What Bibliothecarius did.** Each defect Duc lists was checked directly against **Zwettl, Stiftsbibliothek, Cod. 103** (Zwettl, s. XII 4/4; digitized at manuscripta.at), and the text was corrected from Zwettl at those places. The corrections were then extended to further places where Duc’s printed text agrees with Zwettl against PL. In all there are 107 recorded corrections in 37 of the 92 sections. The readings at Duc’s loci were made in two independent passes on the manuscript images, the extension readings in one pass with Duc as a control, and every correction is logged in an apparatus with PL’s wording, Zwettl’s wording and the folio/line. Duc’s text was used only as a map for locating the defects and was not copied. Where Zwettl could not settle a place, PL was kept and the place is flagged. The Zwettl record confirms that the work occupies fols. 1r–41va: https://manuscripta.at/_scripts/php/msDescription2_m1_NEU.php?ID=31714#:~:text=(1r%2D41va),Opusculum%20de%20expositione%20missae. <a class="cite" href="#ref-5" title="manuscripta.at">[5]</a>

4. **Status.** This is a reading and translation edition built on PL with bounded manuscript corrections. It is **not a critical edition**. A new critical edition with French translation is in progress (Agnès Naud, Sorbonne, thesis begun 2023; theses.fr s382682), and there are no claims about it beyond that: https://theses.fr/s382682#:~:text=Date%20%3A%20Inscription%20en%20doctorat,le%2001%2F09%2F2023. <a class="cite" href="#ref-6" title="Culture patristique et culture eucharistique au Moyen Âge : édition, traduction et commentaire de l'Expositio Missae de Florus de Lyon. | Theses.fr">[6]</a>

5. **Translations.** No complete English translation located. No complete modern French or German translation was located either; Naud’s French translation remains part of the thesis project.

## Author Biography

Florus’s birth date, birthplace, and family are unknown. He was probably born around the end of the eighth century or beginning of the ninth and was active at Lyon approximately **825–855**; his death is usually placed around **860**. These remain approximations rather than documented biographical dates. Contemporaries and the Lyon obituary call him a **deacon** (Wandalbert of Prüm, a subdeacon), *magister*, or Florus of Lyon; only two old manuscripts call him *presbyter*, so if he became a priest it was probably late in life. Duc, pp. 15–16: https://archive.org/details/Duc1937Florus. Chambert-Protat’s research summary uses the deliberately cautious formula *floruit v. 825–855*. <a class="cite" href="#ref-1" title="Full text of &quot;Paul Duc : L'Expositio Missae de Florus de Lyon…&quot;">[1]</a>

He was formed in the intellectual world of Lyon’s cathedral school and library, whose renewal had begun under Bishop Leidrad. Florus subsequently worked through the episcopates of **Agobard, Amolo, and Remigius**. He appears not as an office-holding prelate but as the cathedral church’s exceptionally learned deacon: a teacher, biblical and patristic compiler, poet, canonist, polemicist, textual critic, and organizer or corrector of manuscripts.

His major works and projects include the *Expositio missae*; extensive compilations on the Pauline Epistles from Augustine and twelve other Fathers; canonical collections; a martyrology; poetry; writings connected with episcopal elections and ecclesiastical liberty; the campaign against Amalarius; and works produced during the predestination controversy of the 850s. Modern codicological research has recovered manuscripts that Florus personally read, annotated, corrected, or used to prepare excerpts. This makes him unusually valuable for studying not only Carolingian theology but the practical stages by which a ninth-century scholar turned library books into new compilations. Chambert-Protat’s research profile and publications are listed at https://cv.hal.science/pierre-cp. <a class="cite" href="#ref-7" title="Archive ouverte HAL">[7]</a>

Politically, Florus strongly defended the Lyon church and remained loyal to Agobard during the conflicts surrounding Louis the Pious. Liturgically, he opposed Amalarius’s innovations and speculative interpretations; later, he participated in the Carolingian debates on grace and predestination. His writings can be sharply polemical, but his lasting reputation rests equally on his disciplined use of patristic authorities and his awareness of textual transmission.

Florus’s legacy is therefore twofold. He was an influential Carolingian theologian of the Church, Eucharist, grace, and ecclesiastical order, and he was also an important agent in the survival of patristic literature. Some excerpts copied by Florus preserve evidence for works or textual forms otherwise lost or poorly transmitted. His manuscripts allow modern scholars to examine reading, annotation, excerpting, correction, and editorial construction with unusual precision.
